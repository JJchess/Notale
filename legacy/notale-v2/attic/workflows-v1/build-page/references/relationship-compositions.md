# Relationship-first compositions

Read this reference after identifying the teaching sentence. Pick the smallest geometry that makes the relationship directly visible.

## Process

Use a path, axis, or spatial transformation whose direction is visible.

- Place `3–6` stages on one continuous route.
- Encode state change on the objects, not only in captions.
- Use arrows for causal or directional movement; use an ordered axis for time.
- Attach one key observation to each decisive transition.
- If the learner scrubs or steps, keep previous/current/next states spatially stable.

Useful 1600x900 pattern: compact title strip, a wide central process path, and one bottom instrument/readout lane. Avoid equal cards with stage numbers but no connection.

## Comparison

Use the same coordinate system for both sides.

- Align labels, measures, and baselines row by row.
- Hold scale, crop, time, and viewpoint constant unless the changing variable is the lesson.
- Put the meaningful difference on the shared centerline or in a dedicated difference band.
- Use color only after geometry establishes the comparison.

Useful pattern: shared title, central matched pair or overlaid plot, common axis/legend, and one conclusion callout. Avoid two unrelated illustrations with separate captions.

## Classification

Make parent/child and part/whole relationships visible through enclosure, branching, or indentation.

- Use nested regions for inclusion and a tree for ancestry.
- Keep sibling shapes and visual weight consistent.
- Make the active branch traceable back to its parent.
- Avoid free-floating category cards that appear to be peers when they are not.

Useful pattern: a dominant parent field with two or three nested/branched regions and a side inspector for the selected member.

## Generalization

Make the claim heavier than its supports and connect all evidence to one trunk.

- Place the claim at the visual root.
- Arrange `2–4` supports around one shared line, bracket, or field.
- Encode support type consistently: example, mechanism, observation, or limitation.
- Keep exceptions attached to the claim rather than isolated as footnotes.

Useful pattern: claim in the upper or central anchor, supports hanging below/around a visible trunk, one concluding boundary condition at the edge.

## Exploration

Treat the model as the page, not as an illustration inside a panel.

- Give the model the largest region.
- Attach direct manipulation to meaningful parts; place sliders/toggles beside the property they change.
- Keep live values on the same side as the corresponding labels.
- Show the response in place instead of duplicating it in a separate chart unless the chart reveals a different dimension.
- Give one short instruction phrased as an action and observation.

Useful pattern: model occupying roughly two thirds of the width, a narrow instrument rail, and a claim/readout spanning the remaining lower edge.

## Single focus

Use one image, artifact, question, formula, or number as the dominant field.

- Let annotation follow the object's geometry.
- Keep explanation to one lead and a few attached labels.
- Crop images to preserve the exact feature being taught.
- Use negative space to isolate the target, not to mimic a marketing hero.

## Connector rules

- Start and end lines on real anchors.
- Prefer horizontal/vertical routed paths or simple curves; avoid tangled diagonals.
- Keep a consistent gap between a connector and its label.
- Do not cross text, interactive hit areas, or unrelated boundaries.
- Give labels an intentional quiet field when placed over imagery.
- Recompute SVG or DOM connectors when the controlled geometry changes.
